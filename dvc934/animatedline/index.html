Notes:
  1. We focus on NO2 and PM2.5 as the strongest positive correlates with COVID-19 mortality. Ozone is excluded because it is negatively correlated with COVID-19 deaths, as there is no reason to believe that exposure to ozone would reduce the risk of dying from COVID-19. Instead, ozone is likely to be acting as a proxy for living in very rural areas (where it is more concentrated compared with other pollutants). This air quality map (https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk/data/gis-mapping/) highlights areas of high Ozone exposure, compared with other pollutants.
  2. The purpose of this chart is to show how the relationship in the data between COVID-19 mortality and air pollution changed as the infection spread. This chart is not designed to provide any deeper interpretation of the correlation. However, to explain: the values here are the log natural of the odds ratio of death from COVID-19 for the whole population based on models in which nothing else is controlled for. It is important to remember that an odds ratio does not give an overall risk of death but rather the change in the risk of death from the population baseline; there is no adjustment for infection rate. Finally, we say “exposure” to make it easier to understand what we are measuring is the average concentration of pollutants around the home, which is a good proxy value but not quite the same thing.
